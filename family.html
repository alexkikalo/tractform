Household capacity

Can the people sustain the plan?

Labor, time, food needs, and limits — so systems stay matched to the household.

Labor & time

Hours available beat ideal plans. Count only what the household will actually do every week.

Weekly hours

Sum chore time for animals, garden, and maintenance. Off-farm work and commute cut the real total hard.

Daily non-negotiables

Water, feed, egg collection, milking — things that cannot skip a day. These define minimum staffing.

Peak seasons

Lambing, harvest, hay, processing. If the plan only works with heroic summer weeks, it is oversized.

Backup

Who covers chores when someone is sick or away? Single points of human failure are plan risks.

Food needs

Match production goals to what the household actually eats and will process.

Diet reality

Eggs, meat, milk, and vegetables the household will use beat crops that look good on a self-sufficiency list.

Share from the land

A clear target (e.g. “all eggs, most vegetables, some meat”) is more useful than “as much as possible.”

Preservation

Canning, freezing, curing require time and equipment in peak season. Count that labor as part of production.

Gap plan

What you still buy is not failure. Knowing the gap keeps the system honest and the budget stable.

Realistic limits

Ceilings that protect the household from burnout and failed systems.

Animal ceiling

Maximum animals that fit available hours and backup coverage — not maximum the pasture could theoretically hold.

Garden ceiling

Area you can weed, water, and harvest without resentment. Smaller and continuous beats large and abandoned.

Project load

One major build or system change at a time. Parallel projects are where plans and relationships break.

Growth path

What to add only after a full year of stability. Expansion is earned by capacity, not by enthusiasm alone.
