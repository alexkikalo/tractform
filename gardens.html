Gardens & Crops

Food from the ground

Select a focus for space, water, soil, and labor realities. Set your USDA zone above for climate context.

Space & yield

How much ground you need depends on diet goals and intensity — not on a single magic number.

Kitchen garden

A few hundred square feet of intensive beds can supply significant vegetables for a small household if managed well.

Serious self-feed

Calories (potatoes, corn, beans, grains) need more space and storage. Scale honestly to labor, not just ambition.

Sun & access

Full sun and close to the house beat a large remote patch that gets neglected. Water access is part of “space.”

Expansion path

Start smaller than you think. Add beds only after one full season of success and compost capacity.

Soil & water

Soil quality and irrigation decide whether effort turns into food.

Soil

Texture, drainage, organic matter, and pH. Test before large amendments. Build fertility with compost and cover crops over time.

Water

Consistent moisture beats occasional flooding. Drip or careful hand watering near the house scales better than hoping for rain.

Mulch & cover

Mulch reduces watering and weeds. Cover crops protect and feed soil in the off season.

Hard limits

Rock, contamination, deep shade, or no water rights can make a “garden acre” nearly useless. Verify before committing.

Labor & season

Gardens fail more often from time shortage than from lack of knowledge.

Weekly rhythm

Weeding, watering, and harvest in season are continuous. Size the garden to hours you will actually give every week.

Peak seasons

Spring planting and late-summer processing spike labor. Plan preservation (canning, freezing) as part of the system.

Frost calendar

Last and first frost define the outdoor window. Season extension (cloth, tunnels) adds food and also adds work.

Realistic goal

A reliable supply of high-value crops beats an abandoned “full self-sufficiency” plot. Match ambition to the household.
